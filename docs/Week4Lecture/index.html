Part I: The Centrality Problem

What Is Centrality?

Given a network \(G = (V, E)\), which nodes are most important? Importance is not a single concept — it depends entirely on the mechanism of importance. Five distinct mechanisms, each captured by a different family of measures:

Mechanism The Question Measure Family
Most connected Who has the most direct ties? Degree centrality
Most reachable Who can reach others most quickly? Closeness centrality
Most controlling Who lies on the most shortest paths? Betweenness centrality
Most prestigious Who is connected to other important nodes? Eigenvector / PageRank
Most influential Who can spread information most widely? Katz / diffusion models

Choosing the wrong mechanism for the analytical question produces misleading conclusions. A coordinator in a compartmentalized cell may rank last on degree and first on betweenness; an analyst using only degree will systematically mis-prioritize.

Formal Definition (Freeman, 1978): A centrality index \(C: V \to \mathbb{R}\) assigns a non-negative real number to each node such that higher values indicate greater structural importance according to the chosen mechanism.1

WarningWhy Measure Choice Is an Intelligence Decision

An analyst using degree centrality to prioritize targeting may identify the most socially active actor — who may be a logistics coordinator, a courier, or a social facilitator with no command authority.

The actual operational coordinator may have low degree (deliberate minimization of exposure) but high betweenness (bridges separate cells). Wrong centrality measure → wrong target → wasted operational resources and potentially tipped-off network.

This is not a theoretical concern: the empirical finding in the 9/11 network analysis is one of the most-cited demonstrations of this problem.

NoteKrebs’s 9/11 Finding

Analyzing the hijacker network, Krebs found:

  • Mohamed Atta ranked highest on betweenness centrality — the operational coordinator routing communication between the Hamburg cell, the Florida cell, and Al-Qaeda leadership. This role was invisible from degree alone.
  • Marwan al-Shehhi ranked highest on degree centrality — the most socially active member.

The two roles belonged to different actors. Degree-only analysis would have deprioritized the network’s actual coordinator.2

Centrality Mechanisms — Annotated Example Network

Centralization vs. Centrality

Node centrality is a micro-level score assigned to each individual node. Graph centralization is a macro-level measure of how unequally centrality is distributed across the network. The distinction matters for disruption strategy selection.

Freeman Centralization Index

\[C_X(G) = \frac{\sum_{v \in V} \bigl[C_X(v^*) - C_X(v)\bigr]}{\max_{G' \in \mathcal{G}} \sum_{v \in V} \bigl[C_X(v^*) - C_X(v)\bigr]}\]

where \(v^*\) is the node with the maximum centrality score; the denominator is the maximum possible sum over all graphs on \(n\) nodes (achieved by the star graph \(K_{1,n-1}\), where one hub connects to all others and periphery nodes connect only to the hub).

  • \(C_X(G) = 1.0\): all centrality concentrated in one node (pure star); decapitation optimal
  • \(C_X(G) = 0.0\): all nodes equally central (regular graph); decapitation ineffective
  • Real networks fall between 0 and 1; the value guides disruption strategy selection

The denominator anchors the index to a common scale, enabling comparison across networks of different sizes and across different centrality measures.

TipIntelligence Application: Al-Qaeda Network Transformation

Everton’s analysis of Al-Qaeda’s organizational structure captures the centralization dynamic across an actual disruption campaign:

Pre-2001 — Al-Qaeda showed high betweenness centralization: a small number of senior brokers (notably Bin Laden’s lieutenants in Afghanistan) routed communication and coordination between cells across multiple continents. This made decapitation a viable strategic option, and the 2001–2002 campaign in Afghanistan successfully removed or disrupted several of these high-betweenness nodes.

Post-2003 reorganization — Surviving affiliates deliberately flattened the network structure. Regional affiliates (AQI, AQAP, AQIM) operated with substantially more autonomy. Betweenness centralization dropped significantly. Decapitation of individual nodes became less effective; the network had evolved to make itself more resilient to precisely the strategy that had worked against it.3

Analytical implication: Compute centralization at the start of any sustained disruption campaign. If centralization is declining across time-series analysis, the network is adapting to targeting. Shift strategy accordingly.

Part II: Local Centrality

Degree Centrality

Degree centrality is the simplest and computationally cheapest centrality measure. It captures local connectivity — how active a node is in terms of direct ties — but says nothing about the node’s position in the broader network structure.

Undirected Degree Centrality

\[C_D(v) = \deg(v)\]

Normalized (allows comparison across networks of different sizes):

\[C_D^*(v) = \frac{\deg(v)}{n - 1}\]

where \(n - 1\) is the maximum possible degree in a simple graph on \(n\) nodes. A value of 1.0 means the node is connected to every other node; 0 means isolated.

Directed Degree Centrality

In directed networks, degree splits into two analytically distinct measures:

  • In-degree centrality \(C_D^-(v) = \frac{\deg^-(v)}{n-1}\): measures prestige or popularity — how many others direct ties toward this node. High in-degree suggests authority, leadership, or target-of-information status.
  • Out-degree centrality \(C_D^+(v) = \frac{\deg^+(v)}{n-1}\): measures activity or initiative — how many outgoing connections this node initiates. High out-degree suggests recruiters, propagandists, financiers, or coordinators.

Strength Centrality (Weighted Networks)

When edge weights encode relationship intensity (call frequency, transaction volume, message count), raw degree undercounts activity:

\[C_S(v) = \sum_{u \in N(v)} w(v, u)\]

A node that calls one contact 200 times may be more operationally significant than one with 10 contacts called once each. Strength centrality captures this; degree does not.

WarningWhen Degree Centrality Is Misleading

In compartmentalized dark networks, operational leaders deliberately minimize their degree to reduce exposure risk. They are not the most socially active actors — they are the most carefully connected ones.

Who tends to have high degree in such networks: - Logistics coordinators and couriers (must contact many parties) - Safe house operators (multiple visitors) - Social facilitators and recruiters (broad contact base)

Who tends to have low degree despite high authority: - Cell leaders (contacts only a small number of trusted subordinates and one superior) - Financiers (contact through cutouts) - Planners (isolated from operational layer)

Degree centrality in these networks systematically identifies the support layer, not the command layer. In the Jemaah Islamiyah network analyzed by Everton, the most degree-central actors were largely logistical; operational planners ranked mid-to-low on degree but high on betweenness.4

H-Index and Coreness as Degree Alternatives

Two alternatives to raw degree provide more nuanced pictures of local embedding:

H-index (adapted from citation analysis): Node \(v\) has h-index \(h\) if it has at least \(h\) neighbors each of which has degree \(\geq h\), and no larger \(h\) satisfies this condition. H-index rewards having well-connected neighbors, not just many neighbors — a node with 5 neighbors each of degree \(\geq 5\) has h-index 5, while a node with 10 neighbors each of degree 1 has h-index 1.

Coreness (from \(k\)-core decomposition, covered in Week 3): the coreness of a node equals the largest \(k\)-core it belongs to. Unlike degree, coreness captures the density of the node’s neighborhood recursively — a node with high coreness is embedded in a mutually dense subgraph, not merely attached to many peripheral actors.

Both measures are computable in \(O(m)\) and available in NetworkX (nx.core_number(G)) and Gephi. They are particularly useful as supplements to degree when screening for inner-circle membership in dark networks.

Part III: Path-Based Centrality

Closeness Centrality

Closeness centrality measures how efficiently a node can reach all others — a proxy for information dissemination speed and early warning sensitivity.

Definition

\[C_C(v) = \frac{n-1}{\sum_{u \neq v} d(v, u)}\]

The numerator normalizes the measure so that a star center (the single node optimally placed to reach all others in one step) receives a score of 1.0. Low closeness means the node is peripheral — far from the network’s informational core.

Connected Graph Variants

The standard formula is undefined when the graph is disconnected (the denominator includes \(d(v,u) = \infty\) for unreachable nodes). Two approaches:

  • Wasserman-Faust variant: compute closeness only over reachable nodes, then normalize by the proportion of nodes reachable: \(C_C^{WF}(v) = \frac{n_r - 1}{n - 1} \cdot \frac{n_r - 1}{\sum_{u \in R(v)} d(v,u)}\), where \(n_r = |R(v)|\).
  • Harmonic centrality: sum the reciprocals of all distances, setting \(1/\infty = 0\) by convention:5

\[C_H(v) = \sum_{u \neq v} \frac{1}{d(v, u)}\]

Harmonic centrality is the preferred choice for intelligence network data, which almost always produces disconnected graphs due to collection incompleteness.

Intelligence Applications

  • Identify nodes best positioned for rapid information dissemination (highest closeness = fastest broadcast reach)
  • Identify nodes that would detect threats earliest in a monitoring network (peripheral nodes learn last)
  • Locate early warning sensors: an analyst wanting to monitor a network with limited collection resources should target high-closeness nodes to maximize the fraction of network activity observed

Betweenness Centrality

Betweenness centrality measures how often a node lies on the shortest path between other pairs of nodes. A node with high betweenness controls information flow, can act as a broker or gatekeeper, and represents a structural chokepoint.

Definition

\[C_B(v) = \sum_{\substack{s \neq v \neq t \\ s < t}} \frac{\sigma_{st}(v)}{\sigma_{st}}\]

where:

  • \(\sigma_{st}\) = total number of shortest paths from \(s\) to \(t\)
  • \(\sigma_{st}(v)\) = number of those shortest paths that pass through \(v\) as an intermediate node
  • The ratio \(\sigma_{st}(v)/\sigma_{st}\) is the fraction of geodesics from \(s\) to \(t\) that go through \(v\)
  • Summed over all unordered pairs not including \(v\)

Normalized (divide by the number of possible pairs): for undirected graphs, divide by \(\binom{n-1}{2} = \frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}\).

What Betweenness Captures

  • Brokerage: control over information flowing between otherwise disconnected parts of the network
  • Gatekeeping: ability to filter, delay, modify, or distort messages passing through
  • Structural hole occupancy: nodes bridging gaps between clusters (Burt’s structural holes, Week 3)6
  • Targeted disruption value: removing a high-betweenness node fragments the network far more efficiently than removing a high-degree node in compartmentalized networks

Edge Betweenness

\[C_B(e) = \sum_{s \neq t} \frac{\sigma_{st}(e)}{\sigma_{st}}\]

Edges with high betweenness carry disproportionate network traffic. Severing a high-betweenness edge can fragment the network into disconnected components without requiring the removal of any actor — potentially less detectable operationally.

Computational Complexity

Computed via the Brandes algorithm: \(O(nm)\) for unweighted graphs; \(O(nm + n^2 \log n)\) for weighted. For very large networks (\(n > 10^5\)), approximation algorithms are available. Gephi and NetworkX implement Brandes exactly for intelligence-scale networks (typically \(n < 10^4\)).

WarningThe Betweenness-Targeting Rule

For network disruption in compartmentalized dark networks: target nodes with the highest betweenness, not highest degree.

Betweenness identifies the coordinators and cutouts who route communication across otherwise separate cells — the structural chokepoints. Removing them severs inter-cell pathways even when the cells themselves remain intact.

In flat, redundant networks where many alternative geodesics exist, betweenness becomes less useful as a targeting signal. In those cases, community-level analysis and edge removal may be more effective.

NoteComputational Note

Betweenness is the most computationally expensive of the standard centrality measures at \(O(nm)\), compared to \(O(n + m)\) for degree. For a network with \(n=1{,}000\) nodes and \(m=5{,}000\) edges, Brandes runs in milliseconds on modern hardware. For \(n=100{,}000\), approximate algorithms are recommended. NetworkX defaults to the exact Brandes implementation.

TipKey Insight: Low Degree ≠ Low Betweenness

In the conceptual example above, Node D has degree 2 (the minimum for a non-isolated, non-leaf node on any meaningful path) but maximum betweenness (every path between the two clusters passes through D). This is the archetypal dark network broker profile: minimally connected, maximally controlling.

Part IV: Spectral and Prestige-Based Centrality

Eigenvector Centrality

Degree centrality treats all neighbors equally — a tie to a well-connected hub counts the same as a tie to an isolated peripheral node. Eigenvector centrality corrects this by making each node’s score proportional to the sum of its neighbors’ scores, evaluated recursively.

Definition

\[C_E(v) = \frac{1}{\lambda} \sum_{u \in N(v)} C_E(u)\]

In matrix form: \(A \mathbf{C}_E = \lambda \mathbf{C}_E\)

The centrality vector \(\mathbf{C}_E\) is an eigenvector of the adjacency matrix \(A\), and \(\lambda\) is the corresponding eigenvalue. The Perron-Frobenius theorem guarantees that for connected graphs, the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\) has all positive entries — this is the eigenvector centrality vector.7

Power Iteration (Convergence)

In practice, computed iteratively:

  1. Initialize all nodes with score 1
  2. For each node, replace its score with the sum of its neighbors’ current scores
  3. Normalize the score vector to prevent divergence
  4. Repeat until scores stabilize (convergence criterion: \(\|\mathbf{x}^{(t+1)} - \mathbf{x}^{(t)}\| < \epsilon\))

Approximate eigenvector centrality (6-node example, 4 iterations of power iteration):

Node Score Rank
1 0.68 4
2 1.00 1
3 0.88 2
4 0.86 3
5 0.55 5
6 0.46 6

Node 3 rises to second place (compared to third on degree) because its neighbors include the high-scoring Nodes 1, 2, and 4 — its local neighborhood is prestigious.

WarningLimitation: Disconnected Graphs and Directed Networks

Eigenvector centrality has important failure modes:

  • Disconnected graphs: nodes in isolated components receive score 0 even if they are central within their component. The largest eigenvector has zero entries for unreachable nodes.
  • Directed graphs with sinks: a node that receives edges from high-scoring nodes but has no outgoing edges concentrates score without distributing it. Eventually all score drains into sink components.

Katz centrality and PageRank both address these problems through different mechanisms (see below).

TipIntelligence Application: Arms Broker Networks

In a network linking arms brokers, suppliers, end-users, and financial intermediaries, eigenvector centrality identifies actors who are connected to the most important other actors — not merely the most active ones.

A mid-level broker who has moderate degree but connects directly to major state-level suppliers and NATO-sanctioned end-users will score high on eigenvector centrality. This profile — moderate activity, elite connections — is characteristic of senior facilitators who deliberately minimize their transaction footprint while maintaining access to the network’s core.

Contrast with degree centrality: low-level couriers and logistics coordinators will often have higher degree but lower eigenvector scores, correctly deprioritizing them relative to the facilitator.

Katz Centrality

Katz centrality addresses eigenvector centrality’s failures on directed graphs and disconnected networks by adding a baseline score \(\beta > 0\) to every node regardless of incoming connections:

\[C_K(v) = \alpha \sum_{u} A_{uv} C_K(u) + \beta\]

In matrix form: \(\mathbf{C}_K = \beta(I - \alpha A^\top)^{-1} \mathbf{1}\)

  • \(\alpha\) (attenuation factor): \(0 < \alpha < 1/\lambda_1\). Longer paths contribute exponentially less — indirect influence decays with path length. Setting \(\alpha \to 0\) collapses Katz to a constant; setting \(\alpha \to 1/\lambda_1\) approximates eigenvector centrality.
  • \(\beta\): the baseline score ensuring no node receives zero centrality due to structural isolation. Typically set to 1.

Katz centrality is interpretable as a weighted count of all walks from all other nodes to \(v\), with walks of length \(k\) penalized by \(\alpha^k\). This makes it a measure of how reachable a node is along paths of all lengths, with shorter paths weighted more heavily.8

PageRank

PageRank, developed by Brin and Page for web link analysis, is a damped version of eigenvector centrality designed for directed graphs with disconnected components:

\[PR(v) = \frac{1-d}{n} + d \sum_{u \in \text{in}(v)} \frac{PR(u)}{\deg^+(u)}\]

where \(d \approx 0.85\) is the damping factor — the probability that a random walker follows an outgoing link rather than teleporting to a random node.

  • Each node distributes its PageRank equally among all its out-neighbors
  • The \((1-d)/n\) term prevents score from concentrating in sink nodes (nodes with no outgoing edges) via random teleportation
  • Handles disconnected directed graphs naturally
  • Converges via power iteration to a unique stationary distribution
NoteIntelligence Application: Influence Operations and Information Networks

In a retweet network, nodes are accounts and directed edges represent retweet relationships (A retweets B → B’s content reaches A’s followers). PageRank on this network identifies accounts whose content propagates most widely — not just accounts with many followers (degree), but accounts whose content is amplified by other high-PageRank accounts.

A coordinated influence operation may use a collection of low-follower sock puppet accounts (hub layer) to drive amplification of a small number of high-credibility accounts (authority layer). PageRank of the high-credibility accounts rises because they receive links from many accounts that themselves receive links from others.

In practice: Graphika, DFRLab, and Stanford Internet Observatory have all used eigenvector-family centrality measures (including PageRank variants) as signals in influence operation attribution work.9

HITS: Hubs and Authorities

HITS (Hyperlink-Induced Topic Search), developed by Kleinberg, decomposes centrality into two mutually reinforcing scores — a more nuanced decomposition than PageRank’s single score.10

Algorithm

Authority score \(x(v)\): a node is a good authority if it is pointed to by good hubs.

\[x(v) = \sum_{u \,:\, (u,v) \in E} y(u)\]

Hub score \(y(v)\): a node is a good hub if it points to many good authorities.

\[y(v) = \sum_{u \,:\, (v,u) \in E} x(u)\]

The two equations are mutually recursive: authorities gain score from hubs pointing to them; hubs gain score from the authorities they point to. Computed iteratively until convergence.

Matrix form: Authority vector \(\mathbf{x}\) is the principal eigenvector of \(A^\top A\); hub vector \(\mathbf{y}\) is the principal eigenvector of \(A A^\top\).

HITS vs. PageRank

  • PageRank: single score per node; more incoming links from high-scoring nodes = higher rank
  • HITS: two-dimensional; a node can score high as a hub without being an authority (a news aggregator pointing to many sources), or high as an authority without being a hub (a primary source rarely linking out)
  • Disruption implication: the two roles typically belong to different actors; targeting strategies differ accordingly

HITS: Hubs → Authorities

TipExtremist Network Application

In an online extremist content network:

  • Authorities: accounts that produce original ideological content widely reshared — the ideological producers. Disrupting authorities degrades the quality and volume of content supply.
  • Hubs: accounts that aggregate and amplify content from many authorities, often adding minimal original material — the amplification layer. Disrupting hubs degrades reach and exposure.

The two roles frequently belong to different actors with different operational profiles. HITS identifies both simultaneously; PageRank would conflate them.

Part V: Analytical Application

Comparing Centrality Measures: A Decision Framework

Centrality Measure Selection Guide

Measure Core Question Primary Intelligence Use
Degree Who is most active / connected? Social hubs, recruitment leads, logistics nodes
In-degree Who receives the most incoming ties? Leadership identification, information flow targets
Out-degree Who initiates the most ties? Recruiters, financiers, propagandists
Strength Who has the strongest total relationship intensity? Weighted activity when frequency matters more than count
Closeness Who can reach others fastest? Early warning sensors, rapid dissemination nodes
Betweenness Who controls information flow? Coordinators, cutouts, brokers; disruption targeting
Eigenvector Who is connected to important actors? Elite network membership, prestige identification
PageRank Who receives from high-scoring senders? Influence in directed citation and retweet networks
HITS Hub Who links to many authorities? Amplifiers in information networks
HITS Authority Who is cited by many hubs? Content producers, ideological sources

When Measures Disagree

Disagreement among centrality measures is analytically informative, not a problem to be resolved by picking one measure. Each disagreement pattern carries a structural signature:

  • High betweenness + low degree — classic cutout or broker profile; bridges clusters with minimal direct exposure; high disruption value
  • High degree + low betweenness — local hub within a dense cluster; active but not a bridge; resilient to removal (neighbors remain connected to each other via alternative paths)
  • High eigenvector + low betweenness — well-embedded in a high-status elite cluster but not a structural bridge; removal degrades prestige without fragmenting the network
  • High closeness + low betweenness — centrally located but not a unique pathway; removal does not fragment but increases average path length

Best practice: compute and present all major centrality measures as a ranked table alongside node attributes. Agreement across measures increases confidence in structural importance; disagreement signals a node worth deeper examination.11

Centrality Under Incomplete Data

All intelligence network data is incomplete. Missing nodes and edges systematically distort centrality scores, and the distortion is not uniform across measures.12

Sensitivity Patterns by Measure

Degree centrality — relatively robust to missing edges. If missing data are roughly random and representative, degree rank order is approximately preserved. Fails when collection is systematically biased toward certain node types (e.g., focusing collection on known associates of a small set of seed nodes, inflating their degree artificially).

Betweenness centrality — highly sensitive to missing nodes and edges. A single missing bridge node can radically alter all betweenness scores in the rest of the graph. If the highest-betweenness actor is not yet identified in collection, the second highest-betweenness actor will appear falsely prominent. Betweenness rankings should always be caveated for potential collection gaps.

Closeness centrality — fails or gives misleading results under high incompleteness. Especially problematic in disconnected graphs (collection artifacts produce apparent disconnection that inflates peripheral scores). Use harmonic closeness and report with explicit data completeness flags.

Eigenvector / PageRank — moderately sensitive. Robust if missing data are random; fragile if missing data are systematic. If collection under-samples high-degree nodes (e.g., encrypted messaging platforms reduce visibility of high-volume communicators), eigenvector centrality systematically underestimates scores of adjacent nodes.

Mitigation Strategies

  • Sensitivity analysis: compute centrality after randomly adding or removing 10–20% of edges; observe rank stability. If the top-3 ranking is invariant, findings are moderately robust.
  • Multiple imputation: estimate likely missing edges from attribute similarity, shared affiliations, or network structural models (exponential random graph models); compute centrality on multiple imputed networks; report the distribution of rank scores.
  • Confidence-weighted networks: assign lower edge weights to lower-confidence source reports; compute weighted centrality. This degrades the influence of poorly-sourced ties without removing them entirely.
  • Qualitative validation: triangulate centrality findings against HUMINT or SIGINT characterizations of node roles. If betweenness analysis identifies Node X as a coordinator and independent source reporting describes X as a logistics driver, the discrepancy warrants deeper examination of both the data and the analysis.
WarningRequired Caveat Language

Centrality findings in intelligence products must be accompanied by a data completeness assessment. Model language:

“Node X ranks highest on normalized betweenness centrality (\(C_B^* = 0.42\)) in the reconstructed network. This ranking is based on [N] confirmed relationships from [source types]. Betweenness centrality is highly sensitive to missing bridge nodes; collection gaps in [specific domain or time period] may significantly alter this ranking. Sensitivity analysis across 100 random 15% edge deletions shows rank-1 stability in 73% of trials.”

Targeting and Disruption Logic

Centrality measures directly inform disruption strategy selection. The effectiveness of any strategy depends on the network’s structural properties.

Network Disruption: Before and After Broker Removal

Five Network Disruption Strategies

The appropriate disruption strategy depends on the network’s structural properties — particularly its centralization, density, and degree distribution.

  1. Decapitation — remove highest-betweenness nodes (coordinators and bridges). Most effective in hierarchical, centralized networks. Least effective in flat, distributed networks where multiple alternative paths exist.

  2. Hub removal — remove highest-degree nodes. Most effective in scale-free networks where hubs are rare (power-law degree distribution). Random node removal is ineffective in scale-free networks; targeted hub removal causes rapid collapse.

  3. Edge removal — sever highest-betweenness edges (bridges between clusters). Splits the network into disconnected components without removing actors who may reconstitute links or resurface through other nodes. May be less detectable than node removal.

  4. Peripheral disruption — target low-centrality nodes (recruitment pools, financiers, logisticians). Degrades network capacity and creates friction without triggering immediate reorganization or alerting the core network.

  5. Broker isolation — remove high-betweenness, low-degree nodes (cutouts). Disrupts covert coordination while minimizing evidence that network structure was the targeting criterion, rather than individual attribute information.

TipEmpirical Finding: Jemaah Islamiyah Disruption Simulation

Everton’s simulation analysis of the Jemaah Islamiyah (JI) network in Southeast Asia compared the effectiveness of betweenness-based versus degree-based targeting strategies:

  • Removing the top 10% of nodes by betweenness centrality reduced the giant component size by 43%
  • Removing the top 10% of nodes by degree centrality reduced the giant component size by only 19%

Betweenness-based targeting was more than twice as effective at fragmenting the network as degree-based targeting, using the same number of “actions.”

The finding generalizes: in compartmentalized dark networks that deliberately minimize hub formation (violating the scale-free assumption), betweenness-based targeting consistently outperforms degree-based targeting. In scale-free networks (more common in social media and open organizational networks), degree-based hub removal can be comparably or more effective.13

Centrality in Practice: Gephi and NetworkX

Gephi

  • Statistics panel: one-click computation of degree, betweenness, closeness, eigenvector, PageRank, and modularity (community detection)
  • Results stored immediately as node attributes; directly available for visual encoding
  • Visual encoding workflow: run statistics → open Appearance panel → map centrality attribute to node size or color gradient → export image
  • Export: full centrality table as CSV for reporting and integration with other tools
  • Limitation: treats multigraphs and self-loops inconsistently; verify graph type before running. Betweenness computation uses the Brandes algorithm but may produce unexpected results on directed graphs with unreachable nodes.

i2 Analyst Notebook

  • Built-in Social Network Analysis module computes degree, betweenness, and closeness
  • Integrated with entity attributes, timeline analysis, and link chart visualization
  • Output: ranked node list visualized directly on the chart
  • Standard tool in classified network analysis environments; compatible with US Government classification workflows
  • Does not natively support eigenvector, PageRank, or HITS — use NetworkX for those measures and import results

NetworkX (Python)

import networkx as nx
import pandas as pd

# Load from CSV edge list
G = nx.read_edgelist(
    'data.csv',
    delimiter=',',
    create_using=nx.DiGraph(),
    data=[('weight', float), ('type', str)]
)

# Compute all major centrality measures
results = {
    'degree':       nx.degree_centrality(G),
    'in_degree':    nx.in_degree_centrality(G),
    'out_degree':   nx.out_degree_centrality(G),
    'closeness':    nx.closeness_centrality(G),
    'betweenness':  nx.betweenness_centrality(G, normalized=True),
    'eigenvector':  nx.eigenvector_centrality(G, max_iter=1000),
    'pagerank':     nx.pagerank(G, alpha=0.85),
    'hits_hubs':    nx.hits(G)[0],
    'hits_auth':    nx.hits(G)[1],
    'coreness':     nx.core_number(G.to_undirected()),
}

# Build comparison table
df = pd.DataFrame(results).sort_values('betweenness', ascending=False)
print(df.round(3).to_string())

# Export
df.to_csv('centrality_results.csv')

Full programmatic control; reproducible; scales to networks with millions of nodes using approximate algorithms (nx.betweenness_centrality(G, k=100) for \(k\)-sample approximation).

Lecture Summary

Key Formulas

Degree centrality (normalized)
\(C_D^*(v) = \dfrac{\deg(v)}{n - 1}\)
In/Out-degree centrality
\(C_D^-(v) = \dfrac{\deg^-(v)}{n-1}\); \(\;\;C_D^+(v) = \dfrac{\deg^+(v)}{n-1}\)
Strength centrality
\(C_S(v) = \sum_{u \in N(v)} w(v, u)\)
Closeness centrality
\(C_C(v) = \dfrac{n-1}{\sum_{u \neq v} d(v,u)}\)
Harmonic centrality
\(C_H(v) = \sum_{u \neq v} \dfrac{1}{d(v,u)}\), with \(1/\infty = 0\)
Betweenness centrality
\(C_B(v) = \sum_{s < t,\; s,t \neq v} \dfrac{\sigma_{st}(v)}{\sigma_{st}}\)
Edge betweenness
\(C_B(e) = \sum_{s \neq t} \dfrac{\sigma_{st}(e)}{\sigma_{st}}\)
Freeman centralization index
\(C_X(G) = \dfrac{\sum_v [C_X(v^*) - C_X(v)]}{\max_{G'} \sum_v [C_X(v^*) - C_X(v)]}\)
Eigenvector centrality
\(A\,\mathbf{C}_E = \lambda_1\,\mathbf{C}_E\) (principal eigenvector of \(A\))
Katz centrality
\(\mathbf{C}_K = \beta(I - \alpha A^\top)^{-1}\mathbf{1}\), with \(\alpha < 1/\lambda_1\)
PageRank
\(PR(v) = \dfrac{1-d}{n} + d\!\!\sum_{u \in \text{in}(v)}\!\!\dfrac{PR(u)}{\deg^+(u)}\), \(d \approx 0.85\)
HITS authority
\(x(v) = \sum_{u:(u,v)\in E} y(u)\); converges to eigenvector of \(A^\top A\)
HITS hub
\(y(v) = \sum_{u:(v,u)\in E} x(u)\); converges to eigenvector of \(A\,A^\top\)

Key Analytical Principles

  1. No single centrality measure is universally correct — the choice must be driven by the analytical question (communication speed → closeness; structural chokepoints → betweenness; prestige → eigenvector).
  2. In compartmentalized dark networks, betweenness is the most valuable measure for disruption targeting; degree systematically underperforms and may misidentify support-layer actors as leadership.
  3. Disagreement among measures is analytically informative: high betweenness / low degree = cutout profile; high eigenvector / low betweenness = embedded elite with no bridging role.
  4. Betweenness is the measure most sensitive to data incompleteness — always caveat betweenness findings with a data completeness statement and sensitivity analysis.
  5. Simulate disruption effects before recommending targeting: compute component count, giant component size, and average path length pre- and post-removal; rank alternative targets by disruption effect size.
  6. Network-level centralization informs strategy selection: high centralization → decapitation is viable; low centralization → edge removal, financial disruption, or peripheral attrition may be more effective.
