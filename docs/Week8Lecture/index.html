Part I: Spatial Networks

What Makes a Network Geospatial?

A spatial network is a graph in which nodes are associated with positions in a metric space — typically geographic coordinates — and where edge formation, capacity, or cost is constrained by spatial distance or physical infrastructure.1 The critical distinction from abstract networks: the network is embedded in, not independent of, the physical world.

What Geography Adds to Network Analysis

  • Edge cost grounded in reality: travel time, distance, and logistics cost become edge weights with physical meaning — two nodes 10 km apart via direct road versus across a mountain range have very different effective edge weights
  • Physical constraints on edge existence: borders, terrain, coastlines, rivers, and built infrastructure determine which edges can physically exist; the feasible edge set is geographically bounded, not combinatorially complete
  • Spatial autocorrelation: nodes that are geographically proximate tend to be connected — the probability of a tie decays with distance (Tobler’s First Law)
  • Infrastructure interdependency: physical systems share geographic footprints; a flood, earthquake, or kinetic strike at one location simultaneously affects all co-located networks
  • GEOINT integration: satellite imagery, elevation models, and remote sensing data become node and edge attributes, enabling continuous network update from observed physical activity

Types of Spatial Networks

  • Transportation: road, rail, air, maritime; nodes are intersections or hubs, edges are routes
  • Utility infrastructure: power grids (substations and transmission lines), water (treatment plants and mains), telecommunications (exchanges and fiber routes)
  • Geographic flow networks: migration, trade, and remittance flows between countries or cities
  • Proximity networks: edges connect actors within a specified geographic distance threshold — a common intelligence construction from co-location data
  • Terrain-constrained networks: routes that must traverse specific geographic features — mountain passes, river crossings, maritime straits
NoteWhy Geography Matters for Intelligence

Physical geography determines the feasible space of covert network operations. Smugglers, insurgents, and proliferators are all constrained by geography — they cannot route through mountains, cross bodies of water without infrastructure, or traverse hostile territorial jurisdictions without risk.

Understanding spatial network structure predicts behavioral patterns:

  • Which border crossings must high-volume trafficking use given production origin and destination market?
  • Which geographic nodes are unavoidable chokepoints even for actors who try to avoid them?
  • Where do terrain constraints create predictable convergence of routes from diverse origins?

These questions are answerable through spatial betweenness analysis in ways that purely social network analysis cannot address.

Geographic Chokepoint in a Spatial Network

Planar Graphs and Geographic Constraints

Planar Graphs

A graph is planar if it can be drawn in a plane with no edges crossing. Road networks, pipelines, and most physical infrastructure networks are approximately planar — physical links cannot pass through each other in two-dimensional geographic space.

Euler’s Formula for connected planar graphs:

\[V - E + F = 2\]

where \(V\) = vertices, \(E\) = edges, \(F\) = faces (bounded regions, plus the outer unbounded face). From Euler’s formula, for any simple connected planar graph:

\[E \leq 3V - 6\]

Structural consequences of planarity:

  • Bounded average degree: from \(E \leq 3V - 6\), average degree \(\langle k \rangle < 6\) for any planar graph. Hub nodes with degree \(> 6\) are topologically impossible in a strictly planar network — scale-free degree distributions cannot arise in road or infrastructure networks.
  • No hubs in road networks: a road intersection physically cannot have more than a handful of connecting roads. The preferential attachment mechanisms that produce hubs in social networks are structurally excluded.
  • Betweenness behaves differently: geographic chokepoints — bridges, tunnels, mountain passes, port entrances — acquire high betweenness by physical necessity. Removing them requires physical engineering, not social disruption.2

Waxman Spatial Random Graph Model

The Waxman model provides a null model for spatial networks where edge probability decays exponentially with distance:

\[P(\{u,v\}) = \beta \exp\!\left(-\frac{d(u,v)}{\alpha L}\right)\]

where \(d(u,v)\) is Euclidean distance, \(L\) is the maximum distance in the study area, \(\alpha\) controls distance decay, and \(\beta\) is a scaling factor.3

  • Low \(\alpha\): connections are strongly localized — road networks, criminal territorial networks
  • High \(\alpha\): long-range connections more probable — trade networks, diaspora networks, transnational terrorist networks
WarningWaxman as Intelligence Null Model

The Waxman model is most powerful as a null model for anomaly detection:

  1. Fit a Waxman model to the observed network’s geographic node distribution
  2. Generate 1,000 Waxman null graphs with the same \(n\), \(\beta\), \(\alpha\)
  3. Compare the observed network’s long-range connection frequency against the null distribution

If significantly more long-range connections than Waxman predicts: the network has deliberate transnational reach — a signal of organizational sophistication, external state support, or deliberate capability investment.

If fewer long-range connections: the network is more spatially constrained than geographic proximity would predict — suggesting strong compartmentalization or high operational security discipline.

Al-Qaeda’s network structure showed significantly more long-range connections than a Waxman null model fitted to Muslim-majority population geography would predict — consistent with deliberate cultivation of transnational coordination that exceeded what organic proximity-driven network formation could explain.

NoteFive Eyes as Waxman Anomaly

The Five Eyes intelligence-sharing arrangement (US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand) represents a positive anomaly in international intelligence cooperation. Geographic distance and linguistic proximity predict some connections, but the strength and institutional breadth of Five Eyes significantly exceeds Waxman null model predictions — evidence of deliberate institutional design overriding pure spatial constraints.

Key Spatial Network Metrics

Circuity (Detour Factor)

\[\eta(u,v) = \frac{d_G(u,v)}{d_E(u,v)}\]

where \(d_G\) is shortest path length weighted by geographic distance, and \(d_E\) is straight-line Euclidean distance.4

  • \(\eta = 1.0\): perfectly straight routing (great-circle maritime routes in open ocean)
  • \(\eta \approx 1.2\)–\(1.4\): typical well-developed road network
  • \(\eta > 1.5\): significant geographic detour — terrain barriers, poor infrastructure, or deliberate routing around monitored areas

High circuity on a known trafficking route implies actors are accepting significant logistical cost — suggesting lower-circuity alternatives are perceived as higher-risk due to enforcement or territorial control.

Network Efficiency

\[E = \frac{1}{n(n-1)} \sum_{u \neq v} \frac{1}{d_G(u,v)}\]

with convention \(1/\infty = 0\) for disconnected pairs.5 Infrastructure attack effectiveness: \(\Delta E = E_{\text{before}} - E_{\text{after}}\).

Spatial Betweenness

Standard betweenness weighted by geographic path length rather than hop count. Identifies nodes that are both topologically central and geographically unavoidable — the most actionable interdiction targets.

Part II: Multi-Layer and Multiplex Networks

Limitations of Single-Layer Analysis

Real-world actors maintain multiple, qualitatively different relationship types simultaneously: kinship, financial transactions, encrypted communication, organizational co-membership, and geographic proximity. Collapsing all of these into a single layer — or analyzing each in isolation — systematically loses critical structural information.6

Analytical Failures of Single-Layer Approaches

Aggregation makes edges incommensurable: merging a kinship tie (strong, persistent) with a single financial transaction (weak, possibly operational security noise) into one weighted edge produces a meaningless composite with no clear interpretation.

Layer-by-layer analysis misses correlations: a node peripheral in every individual layer may be central when layers are considered jointly. An actor with 5 unique contacts in communication, 3 different unique contacts in finance, and 2 more in kinship has access to 10 unique actors — far more than any single-layer view reveals.

Centrality is layer-specific: the key financial coordinator may have low centrality in the communication network. Single-layer targeting based on one relationship type systematically misses actors whose importance is distributed across types.

Cascade effects are invisible: a failure in one infrastructure layer propagates to dependent layers in ways structurally invisible to any single-layer analysis.

TipA Multi-Layer Illustration

Three actors A, B, C:

  • Layer 1 (Communication): A–B, B–C (B is the hub)
  • Layer 2 (Financial): A–C only (B is absent; A and C transact directly)
  • Layer 3 (Kinship): A–C (family relationship)

Single-layer: B is central in communication; appears peripheral in finance and kinship.

Multi-layer: A is most broadly connected across all three layers. B is a communication specialist. The A–C dyad carries a multi-strand tie (financial + kinship) far more resilient than any single-strand tie.

Targeting implication: disrupting B leaves A–C coordination fully intact across financial and kinship channels — making the B-targeting strategy operationally ineffective despite its communication-layer prominence.

Formal Multilayer Network Framework

Kivelä et al. (2014) Formalization

A multilayer network:

\[\mathcal{M} = (G, C, \mathbf{X}_A, \mathbf{X}_L, \mathbf{X}_V, \mathbf{X}_E)\]

where \(G\) = set of layer graphs; \(C\) = interlayer couplings; \(\mathbf{X}\) = attribute functions on aspects, layers, vertices, and edges.

Key terminology:

  • Layer \(\alpha\): a single graph \(G^\alpha = (V^\alpha, E^\alpha)\) — one relationship type
  • Node-layer pair \((v, \alpha)\): node \(v\)’s presence in layer \(\alpha\)
  • Intra-layer edge: tie between two nodes within the same layer
  • Inter-layer edge: coupling of node \(v\) across layers \((v,\alpha) \to (v,\beta)\)
  • Aspect: the dimension along which layers differ (type, time, scale)

Special Cases

  • Multiplex network: same node set \(V\) across all layers; only edge types differ. Most common in intelligence applications.
  • Interconnected network: different node sets per layer with inter-layer edges between different nodes
  • Temporal network: layers = time slices; inter-layer edges connect node at \(t\) to same node at \(t+1\)
  • Spatial-temporal network: layers indexed by both space and time

Supra-Adjacency Matrix and Multilayer Centrality

Supra-Adjacency Matrix

For a multiplex network with \(n\) nodes and \(L\) layers, the supra-adjacency matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) is an \(nL \times nL\) block matrix:7

\[\mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} A^{(1)} & I\omega_{12} & \cdots & I\omega_{1L} \\ I\omega_{21} & A^{(2)} & \cdots & I\omega_{2L} \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ I\omega_{L1} & \cdots & \cdots & A^{(L)} \end{pmatrix}\]

where \(A^{(\alpha)}\) is the \(n \times n\) adjacency matrix of layer \(\alpha\); \(I\) is the identity matrix; and \(\omega_{\alpha\beta}\) is the coupling weight between layers \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\).

Interpretation: each node-layer pair \((v, \alpha)\) connects to its within-layer neighbors via \(A^{(\alpha)}\) and to its own counterpart in all other layers via \(I\omega\).

Coupling Weight \(\omega\)

The coupling weight determines how strongly node identity links across layers — a modeling choice:

  • High \(\omega\): position in one layer strongly influences others — appropriate when the same relationship type spans layers
  • Low \(\omega\): layers operate nearly independently — appropriate when relationship types are qualitatively distinct
  • Sensitivity analysis over \(\omega\) values is mandatory before any multilayer finding appears in a finished intelligence product

Multilayer Centrality Measures

  • Degree versatility \(o_v\): how uniformly node \(v\) participates across layers; \(o_v = 1\) means equal participation; low versatility = layer-specific specialist
  • Multilayer betweenness: summed betweenness across all layers, or computed on the full supra-adjacency graph
  • Eigenvector centrality on supra-adjacency: identifies nodes important across all layers simultaneously
  • PageRank on the supra-graph: random walker moves within and between layers according to coupling weights
WarningCoupling Weight Choices

There is no universal coupling weight \(\omega\) applicable to all intelligence problems. For a proliferation network, coupling the technical and financial layers with equal weight assumes that technical relationships translate equally to financial influence — an assumption that requires explicit analytical justification.

Standard practice: run analyses at \(\omega = 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0\). Report findings stable across all values as high-confidence; findings sensitive to \(\omega\) as model-dependent and requiring additional validation.

Multiplex Structural Measures

Edge Overlap

The fraction of layers in which a node pair is simultaneously connected:

\[o_{ij} = \frac{\sum_\alpha A_{ij}^\alpha - 1}{L - 1} \in [0, 1]\]

  • \(o_{ij} = 0\): connected in exactly one layer — single-strand tie; fragile; layer-specific disruption severs it entirely
  • \(o_{ij} = 1\): connected in every layer — fully redundant multi-strand tie; persists as long as any one layer remains intact

Layer Correlation

Pearson correlation of degree vectors across layers \(\rho_{\alpha\beta} = \text{Pearson}(\mathbf{k}^\alpha, \mathbf{k}^\beta)\):

  • High positive \(\rho\): the same actors are central across relationship types — generalist hubs; robust, consistent importance
  • Low or negative \(\rho\): different actors dominate in different layers — specialists; layer-specific importance, potentially disruption-sensitive
TipMulti-Strand Ties in Counterproliferation

In counterproliferation network analysis, a relationship appearing simultaneously in the financial transaction network, the communication network, the physical co-location network, and the procurement network has edge overlap \(o_{ij} \approx 1.0\) — fully multi-strand and extraordinarily resilient to single-layer disruption.

The A.Q. Khan proliferation network featured exactly this pattern between Khan and his key technical partners: simultaneous commercial, technical, financial, and political protection relationships ensured that sanctioning one party (disrupting the commercial layer) left the technical and financial layers fully intact.

Analytical recommendation: rank all node pairs by edge overlap. Highest-overlap dyads represent the most durable partnerships — the structural backbone of persistent adversary relationships.

Part III: Tools and Workflows

ArcGIS Network Analyst: Capabilities and Workflow

Core Capabilities

Esri’s Network Analyst extension enables spatial network analysis embedded in ArcGIS Pro, with direct integration of basemaps, satellite imagery, and GEOINT data layers.

Route analysis: Shortest path between origin and destination, respecting time-of-day travel speeds, restricted turns, one-way constraints, and turn impedance. Configurable impedance: travel time, distance, cost, or composite risk-weighted cost.

Service area analysis: Polygons of all areas reachable from a facility within specified travel time or distance. Reveals operational reach: which population centers, border crossings, or infrastructure nodes lie within two-hour driving distance of a suspected command location?

Origin-Destination (OD) matrix: Pairwise shortest-path distances and times between all origins and destinations. Output is a weighted directed edge list — the bridge between ArcGIS spatial analysis and NetworkX/Gephi SNA.

Closest facility: For each demand point, identifies the nearest facility. Intelligence application: given known cache locations, which cache is closest to each potential demand point for weapons, material, or personnel?

Location-allocation: Optimally selects facility locations to minimize aggregate travel cost. Applicable to: optimal base placement for patrol coverage, optimal interdiction point selection to cover the most trafficking routes.

NoteIntelligence Workflow: Arms Cache Service Areas

Scenario: from 12 confirmed and suspected arms cache locations in a conflict zone, determine which road network nodes are within 2-hour and 6-hour driving distances.

ArcGIS workflow:

  1. Import cache locations as point features; connect to road network dataset
  2. Run Service Area analysis: 2- and 6-hour drive-time polygons from each cache
  3. Compute polygon union/intersection: identify overlap zones where multiple caches can reach the same destination within the same time window
  4. Overlay with critical infrastructure, population centers, and known target sites
  5. Rank infrastructure nodes by number of caches within their 2-hour service area
  6. Export ranked node list as OD matrix input for further network analysis

The convergence zone map identifies areas simultaneously accessible from multiple independently positioned caches — the highest-threat convergence points as collection and watch priorities.

Network Dataset Construction

Built from polyline features (roads from OSM, HERE, NGA VMAP) split at intersections. Impedance attributes: travel time, distance, cost, or analyst-assigned risk rating. Turn and edge restrictions model border closures, restricted zones, and terrain impassability.

OSMnx and GeoPandas

OSMnx: Street Network Analysis in Python

OSMnx (Boeing 2017) acquires, models, analyzes, and visualizes street networks from OpenStreetMap data as NetworkX MultiDiGraph objects.8

import osmnx as ox
import networkx as nx

# Download drive network for an area of interest
G = ox.graph_from_place(
    'Kandahar, Afghanistan',
    network_type='drive'
)

# Network statistics (circuity, intersection density)
stats = ox.basic_stats(G)
print(f"Circuity: {stats['circuity_avg']:.3f}")

# Betweenness centrality = geographic chokepoints
btw = nx.betweenness_centrality(
    G, weight='travel_time', normalized=True
)
top_nodes = sorted(btw, key=btw.get, reverse=True)[:5]

# Shortest path (geolocated points)
orig = ox.nearest_nodes(G, X=65.707, Y=31.613)
dest = ox.nearest_nodes(G, X=65.720, Y=31.625)
route = ox.shortest_path(
    G, orig, dest, weight='travel_time'
)

# Export to GeoDataFrame for ArcGIS
nodes_gdf, edges_gdf = ox.graph_to_gdfs(G)
nodes_gdf['betweenness'] = nodes_gdf.index.map(btw)
nodes_gdf.to_file('network_centrality.gpkg')

GeoPandas: Proximity Network Construction

import geopandas as gpd
import numpy as np
from sklearn.neighbors import BallTree
import networkx as nx

# Create GeoDataFrame from actor coordinates
gdf = gpd.GeoDataFrame(
    actors,
    geometry=gpd.points_from_xy(
        actors.lon, actors.lat),
    crs='EPSG:4326'
)

# BallTree haversine for spherical distances
coords_rad = np.radians(
    gdf[['lat', 'lon']].values
)
tree = BallTree(coords_rad, metric='haversine')

# Connect actors within 50 km threshold
r_rad = 50 / 6371  # 50 km in radians
indices = tree.query_radius(coords_rad, r=r_rad)

# Build edge list (undirected, no self-loops)
edges = [
    (actors.iloc[i]['id'], actors.iloc[j]['id'])
    for i, nbrs in enumerate(indices)
    for j in nbrs if i < j
]

G = nx.Graph()
for _, row in gdf.iterrows():
    G.add_node(row['id'],
               pos=(row.lon, row.lat))
G.add_edges_from(edges)

The resulting proximity graph connects actors by geographic co-location — a baseline for distinguishing geographically-driven from organizationally-driven clustering.

Part IV: Intelligence Applications

Smuggling and Trafficking Route Analysis

Network Model

  • Nodes: origin production zones, transit points (border crossings, ports, intermediary cities, distribution hubs), destination markets
  • Edges: directed, weighted by estimated volume or frequency; multiple commodities (narcotics, weapons, human beings, wildlife) may traverse the same physical network with different route optimizations
  • Structural logic: smuggling networks exploit structural holes in enforcement coverage and follow the path of least enforcement resistance — not necessarily the geographically shortest path

Key analytical questions:

  • Which transit nodes have highest betweenness? → Priority interdiction targets
  • What alternative routes exist if the highest-betweenness node is interdicted? → Circuity cost of rerouting; resilience assessment
  • Are there spatial communities in the route network corresponding to distinct trafficking organizations?
  • How do routes respond to enforcement pressure? → Temporal analysis (Week 8)

Andean Cocaine Trafficking Route Network

WarningData Challenge: Enforcement Selection Bias

Observed seizure data is biased toward enforcement-heavy routes. Routes showing the most seizures are precisely those with the heaviest interdiction coverage — not necessarily the highest-volume routes. True high-volume routes may produce fewer seizures because enforcement resources are directed elsewhere.

The Afghanistan heroin trafficking network demonstrates this: the highest-volume routes through Iran produced relatively few seizures compared to the Balkans route that received more interdictions per kilogram shipped — a direct consequence of differential enforcement investment, not differential volume.

Analytical mitigation: combine seizure data with satellite imagery vehicle counts, financial intelligence on cartel revenue and market consumption estimates, HUMINT on route prices (higher prices = scarcer, better-protected; lower prices = high-volume, low-risk), and court record reconstructions from prosecuted trafficking cases.

Critical Infrastructure Interdependency

The Interdependency Problem

Modern critical infrastructure systems are deeply interdependent: power grids require telecommunications for operational control; telecom requires power; water treatment requires power and chemical supply; financial systems require telecom and power.9 This creates cascading failure risk invisible to single-layer analysis.

Four-layer interdependency model:

Layer Nodes Depends on
1: Power grid Substations, generators (primary)
2: Telecom Exchanges, towers Layer 1
3: Water Treatment plants, pumps Layers 1 + 2
4: Transportation Road/rail nodes Layer 1 (signals)

Cascading failure sequence:

  1. Target a specific substation in Layer 1
  2. Remove it; identify Layer 1 nodes that disconnect from generators
  3. Identify Layer 2 nodes powered by failed Layer 1 nodes — they fail
  4. Identify Layer 3 nodes dependent on failed Layer 2 nodes — they fail
  5. Compute total fraction of each layer that fails from the initial single-node attack

Cascading Infrastructure Failure

WarningBuldyrev et al. (2010): The Phase Transition

Interdependent networks exhibit a first-order phase transition at a critical failure threshold — not the gradual degradation of single-layer networks. A random failure rate of 40% in a single-layer network produces modest damage. The same failure rate in two interdependent layers can trigger total network collapse.10

The 2015 and 2016 Ukrainian power grid attacks (Sandworm/GRU) demonstrated this empirically: remote substation attacks combined with SCADA disruption to deny restoration — the first documented cyberattacks to trigger deliberate infrastructure cascade. The 2003 Northeast blackout (50 million people across US and Canada) showed the same dynamics from a software fault, propagating through interconnected power systems far beyond the initial triggering event.

Multi-Layer Analysis of State Actor Networks

State Actor Networks as Multiplex Structures

State and quasi-state actors operate simultaneously across multiple relationship layers. Multiplex analysis enables:

  • Identifying multi-strand partnerships present across diplomatic, military, economic, and intelligence layers simultaneously — the most durable and strategically significant bilateral ties
  • Predicting which relationships survive disruption to one layer (sanctions, diplomatic rupture, regime change)
  • Distinguishing formal from informal relationships: treaty-based cooperation vs. intelligence-to-intelligence arrangements operating without formal diplomatic acknowledgment
  • Locating structural holes in expected relationship networks: bilateral relationships that should exist given shared threat environments or economic complementarity but do not

Illustrative layer set for state actor multiplex analysis:

  1. Diplomatic recognition and treaty network
  2. Arms transfer network (SIPRI arms transfer database — open source)
  3. Trade and investment flows (IMF DOTS, World Bank bilateral trade)
  4. Intelligence cooperation (Five Eyes, bilateral liaison arrangements)
  5. Military basing and access agreements (SOFA registrations)
  6. UN General Assembly voting similarity (broad political alignment proxy)
TipA.Q. Khan Network: Four-Layer Multiplex

The A.Q. Khan proliferation network (1987–2004) operated simultaneously across four qualitatively distinct layers:11

Layer 1 — Commercial: front companies (SMB Group, Gulf Technical Industries), export licenses through European and Gulf intermediaries, legitimate manufacturing relationships for dual-use components.

Layer 2 — Technical: nuclear weapons design and centrifuge technology transfer between Pakistani, Libyan, Iranian, and North Korean engineers.

Layer 3 — Financial: shell company transactions across Swiss, Dubai, South African, and Malaysian financial systems with multi-hop wire transfers to obscure origin.

Layer 4 — Political protection: Pakistani ISI cover, diplomatic interference with IAEA inspections, deliberate compartmentalization from Pakistani foreign ministry.

The multilayer finding: disrupting the commercial layer (export controls, sanctions on front companies) did not sever technical or financial layers, which continued through alternative channels. Multi-strand architecture provided structural resilience that single-layer interdiction could not overcome.

WarningData Availability Warning

Classified layers (intelligence cooperation, political protection) are rarely available in open-source analysis. Analysts must clearly distinguish data-rich layers from speculative layers, and present findings accordingly.

Integrating GEOINT and Network Data

GEOINT as Network Input

  • Facility detection: satellite imagery identifies physical nodes — warehouses, refineries, military installations, border crossings. GEOINT provides node locations, physical dimensions, and activity status as node attributes.
  • Activity monitoring: change detection over time reveals when nodes become operationally active; vehicle/vessel presence indicates operational status; this drives temporal network updates (Week 8).
  • Route corroboration: observed vehicle tracks, vessel AIS trails, and flight data provide ground-truth validation for proposed spatial network edges.
  • Infrastructure mapping: pipeline routes, power line corridors, and road networks extracted from imagery constitute the physical edge layer of infrastructure multiplex networks.
  • Geographic context: terrain, urban density, and border architecture define the spatial constraints within which network edges are physically possible.

Network Analysis as GEOINT Enhancement

  • Collection prioritization: high-betweenness nodes identified by network analysis become priority imagery collection targets; structural importance directs sensor tasking.
  • Unknown node inference: if actors A and C are both network-connected to an unobserved node B (inferred from traffic patterns or financial flows), network analysis predicts B’s existence and constrains its geographic location within the spatial network.
  • Route prediction: spatial betweenness identifies which geographic corridors illicit actors must use given their origin-destination pairs and terrain constraints — enabling anticipatory ISR positioning.
  • Activity baseline: expected traffic volume at a high-betweenness geographic node; anomaly detection when observed imagery traffic deviates from structural prediction.
NoteThe GEOINT-SNA Synergy

SNA identifies which locations are structurally important; GEOINT characterizes what is happening at those locations.

In practice: run network analysis first to generate a priority target list ranked by structural metrics; direct GEOINT collection against that list; use collection findings to update the network model; iterate. The combination is more powerful than either approach alone.

\(\mathcal{M} = (G, C, \mathbf{X}_A, \mathbf{X}_L, \mathbf{X}_V, \mathbf{X}_E)\) (Kivelä et al. 2014)
Supra-adjacency matrix
Block \(nL \times nL\) matrix; diagonal blocks \(A^{(\alpha)}\) (intra-layer); off-diagonals \(I\omega_{\alpha\beta}\) (coupling)
Edge overlap
\(o_{ij} = (\sum_\alpha A_{ij}^\alpha - 1)/(L-1) \in [0,1]\); \(o_{ij}=1\) = fully multi-strand tie

Key Analytical Takeaways

  1. Geographic chokepoints in spatial networks are identified by betweenness centrality but are physically constrained — they cannot be bypassed without significant circuity cost. This is analytically distinct from social network chokepoints, which can be circumvented through organizational adaptation.

  2. Multiplex analysis reveals multi-strand ties (high edge overlap) completely invisible to single-layer analysis. These are the most durable and analytically significant relationships in any target network.

  3. Infrastructure interdependency creates catastrophic cascade failure risk far exceeding what single-layer analysis predicts. A single physical attack on a high-betweenness power node can produce threefold or greater infrastructure damage through cascade.

  4. GEOINT and SNA are mutually reinforcing: SNA directs imagery collection toward structurally important nodes; imagery grounds the network model and reveals physical activity at predicted chokepoints.

  5. Coupling weight \(\omega\) between multilayer layers is always a modeling choice; sensitivity analysis over a range of \(\omega\) values is required before multilayer findings appear in finished intelligence products.
